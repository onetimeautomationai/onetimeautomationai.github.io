Your Brain Holds Seven Things. Your AI Does Not.

By item eight on my grocery list, I have lost item two. AI memory is not a bigger version of mine. It works on different rules, and I spent months treating it like it had my limits.

Leo Chen · 4 min read · AI memory, part two

Every week I write my grocery list in my head. Oatmeal, eggs, bagels, kefir. By the time I hit item eight, I have forgotten what item two was.

Nothing wrong with me. That is what a human brain does when it tries to hold more than about seven things at once. It is the reason people give for phone numbers landing on seven digits in the first place.

So how does that differ from how AI handles memory?

The moment it stopped being theoretical

I was deep into a client project, juggling five different threads, and I asked my agent to pull up something complicated I had mentioned three weeks earlier in a completely different conversation.

It just knew it. Word for word. No re-explaining, no scrolling back through old messages myself.

That is the moment the difference stopped being a spec sheet number and became a working difference.

It is not just bigger. It is a different kind.

My memory reconstructs. It fades. It fills in gaps without telling me, unconsciously and confidently, and I have no way to tell a real recollection from a smooth repair job.

My AI's memory does not reconstruct anything. It has the exact factual thing, sitting there, the way it was actually said. And it does not stop at seven.

Mine reconstructs and does not tell me. Its does not reconstruct at all.

The size gap is real too. Context windows on current models run to about a million tokens, which is roughly three quarters of a million words. But size is the less interesting half. The interesting half is that nothing quietly rots in there while you are not looking.

The mistake I made for months

I treated it like it had my limits.

Short notes. Quick asks. A fresh start every session, like it was going to forget the way I forget. Re-explaining my own business at the top of every conversation because surely it could not be holding all of that.

That is my limit, not the agent's. And every time I opened a clean session to be safe, I threw away the context that made the answer good.

Truth is, it never forgets unless somebody sets it up to. That is the whole difference.

What to do with that

Stop paying the re-explaining tax. Three moves, in order of how much they give back:

  • Write the business down once. Who you are, what you sell, who your people are, how you talk. One file. Point every session at it instead of retyping the preamble.
  • Stop opening fresh sessions out of politeness. A long thread is not a burden on it. Continuity is the asset.
  • Let it read the source, not your summary. Every summary you write by hand is you compressing something down to fit a limit that is not there.

None of that is a tool purchase. It is a habit change, and it is the one that makes every prompt in this library work better than it does on a cold start.
