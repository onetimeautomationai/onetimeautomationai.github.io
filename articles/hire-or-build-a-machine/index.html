Hire, or Build a Machine

Every business hits the same wall: more work than hours. There are two answers to that, and almost nobody uses the second one.

Leo Chen · 4 min read · How I decide

More work than hours. So you go find a person.

You write the job post. You read forty applications. You interview five. You pick one. You train them for six weeks. Somewhere in month three they are finally useful.

Then they get a better offer and you start over.

I have done this. It works. It is also the slowest possible answer to the sentence "I need this handled."

There is a second answer. Almost nobody uses it. It sounds like science fiction right up until you watch it run.

Some work should never have been a person's job

Not because people are not worth it. Because that work is not worth a person.

Reading every email to decide which ones matter is not worth a person. Neither is pulling the same numbers into the same report every Monday. Neither is sending the follow up you promised sixteen days ago and forgot.

Those three have something in common. They repeat. They have rules. And they punish you badly when you skip them.

That is not a job. That is a machine nobody has built yet.

The difference in one line

A hire costs you money every month and gets tired. A machine costs you one decision and runs at 3 AM on a holiday.

You hire once and manage forever. You install once and it is finished.

That is not a knock on hiring. It is a statement about where the ongoing cost sits. A person carries a recurring cost in money and, more expensively, in your attention. Every hire adds a line to the list of things you check on. A machine adds one decision at the front and then goes quiet.

What this does not mean

It does not mean fire anyone. My team does more of the work only a human can do, not less. What changed is that the work only a machine should do stopped going to a human.

It does not mean you become technical. You decide what the job is. Somebody else can set it up.

You are the driver. You do not have to be the builder.

The test I use now

Before I hand anything to a person, I ask one question.

Does this need judgment, or does it just need to happen?

If it needs judgment, a person. Every time. Judgment is the thing people are actually good at and machines are actually bad at, and pretending otherwise is how you end up with a confident answer that is wrong in a way nobody catches for a month.

If it just needs to happen, it should never touch a human calendar again. Consistency is the one thing a person is worst at. You have good days and bad days and a kid with a fever. A machine has Tuesday.

Run it on your own week once

Most business owners never have. Run it once and it is uncomfortable how much lands on the second side.

The catch is that you cannot run the test from memory. You will remember the two hard calls and forget the thirty small things, and the thirty small things are the entire answer. You need the week read back to you by something with no reason to be kind about it.
