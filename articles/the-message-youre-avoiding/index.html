The Message You Have Been Sitting On

What stops you is almost never the words. It is the answer you might get back.

Leo Chen · 4 min read · Prompt of the Week 10

This might have happened to you.

Two years ago I closed a great deal for a couple. I should have asked for a referral right after. I never did. It felt awkward, bringing it up out of nowhere.

I sat on that message for over a week. Told myself I would send it tomorrow. Every day.

When I finally did, they had a friend who had been looking for six months. One text, one referral, one closing.

You probably have one right now

Maybe it is the seller who needs to hear the price is too high. Maybe it is the buyer who went quiet after the third showing. Maybe it is the referral ask you have never actually sent, to somebody who would probably say yes.

Outside real estate it looks the same. The invoice that is forty days late. The client who needs to hear that the scope changed. The partner conversation you have rehearsed in the shower nine times and never had.

They all share one shape. Small to write. Heavy to send.

What waiting actually costs

The number gets worse or the lead gets colder every day you sit on it. That part is obvious.

The part people miss is the second cost. Once you have waited, you also have to explain why you waited. A price conversation on day three is a price conversation. On day twenty-four it is a price conversation plus an apology, and the apology is the harder half.

What is stopping you is almost never the words. You are afraid of the answer you might get.

Which is worth naming, because it changes what would actually help. A better template does not fix fear. Knowing what comes back does.

So I built a prompt for it

Two of them, really, and they do different jobs.

The first drafts the message. Plainly, in your voice, short enough to send from a phone, without the four paragraphs of throat-clearing people write when they are nervous.

The second is the one that actually gets it sent. It tells you which version to send and what to expect back, including the responses you are quietly dreading, so the reply is not a surprise when it lands. Rehearsing the answer is what makes the send survivable.

Neither one sends anything. They draft for your review. You press the button.

The two-minute version. Write the message badly, on purpose, in one line. Then hand that line over and ask for the sendable version plus the three likely replies. Most of what keeps a message unsent dies the moment you can see the replies written down.

The one you are sitting on

Pick it now, before you close this. Not the list of them. The single one that came to mind in the first sentence of this piece, because that one is the real one.

Mine sat for a week. It was worth a closing.
