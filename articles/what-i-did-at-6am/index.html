I Checked On My Agent at 6am on a Saturday

Nothing was wrong. I knew nothing would be wrong. I watched it anyway, the way you check on a kid at the pool.

Leo Chen · 4 min read · Productivity addiction

Saturday, 6am. I was not working. I opened my email anyway.

Twenty messages were sitting in my inbox. My agent sorted them in about four seconds, filed and done before I had had coffee. I watched it happen and smiled at my phone like it had done something impressive.

It does this every morning. I have seen it a thousand times.

I sat there checking on it the way you would check on a kid at the pool, even though nothing was wrong and I knew it would not be.

A few of the ones I still watch

  • The weekly client listing report that packages itself by morning.
  • The four things I told a client I would do on a phone call, logged and started without me writing anything down.
  • The calendar that books off a text message confirmation to a client.
  • The weekly finance report sitting on my desk every Friday morning.

Yes, it is time saved, and that was the point. But it also feels like a show I want to watch.

Trust but verify, as a habit you cannot drop

If you have ever built something that does a real piece of your job, correctly, as instructed, you already know the feeling. There is a pull to check on it anyway.

The dopamine hit when it is right again.

It is fun. It is also a little bit addictive.

Worth being honest about, because it has a cost. Checking a thing that works is still time, and the whole point was to get the time back. The verify habit is right at the start, when you genuinely do not know whether the rule holds. Somewhere after the fiftieth clean run it stops being verification and starts being entertainment.

The useful version of that instinct is not watching. It is building the check into the thing itself, so it tells you when something looks wrong instead of you going to look. A job that reports its own failures does not need an audience.

So one weekend I mapped them all like employees

Not as a diagram exercise. Because I had lost track of what I was actually running, and a list of scripts does not tell you that.

Drawn as an org chart it tells you immediately. Who reports to what. Which roles are filled and which are one person doing three jobs. Where the single points of failure sit. The same things an org chart tells you about a company with people in it.

The exercise is worth doing before you have twenty of them, not after. Once you can see the chart, the next hire is obvious, and so is the role you have been quietly doing yourself because nobody was assigned to it.

Try it at four. You do not need a big system for this to work. Write down the four things that already run without you and draw who they report to. The gaps show up immediately, and the gaps are the roadmap.
